MRM Maturity Framework — Deterministic Models to LLMs

A maturity assessment and progression framework tracking how bank Model Risk Management practices must evolve as institutions move from deterministic models, through machine learning, to LLMs and generative AI. Grounded in PRA SS1/23, SR 11-7, EU AI Act, and NIST AI RMF.

Framework overview

Read this grid as the framework's skeleton: each row is a control discipline every bank already runs for deterministic models; the labels show illustratively how the same discipline is typically operated at each technology era, getting harder left to right. The Maturity Levels tab turns each cell into a scoreable 1-5 statement; Regulatory Mapping and Worked Examples then ground each level in the four source frameworks and concrete illustrations.

Dimension rationale & regulatory anchoring

1. Governance & Accountability

Board-level ownership of model risk appetite, an accountable senior individual, and policies that scale across the firm. This is the cross-cutting foundation — without it, every other dimension lacks teeth.

SS1/23 Principle 2SR 11-7 Pillar 3EU AI Act Art.9(9)NIST Govern

2. Inventory & Risk Classification

A single source of truth for what models/AI systems exist, and a risk-based tiering approach so effort is proportionate. Becomes materially harder as ML/LLM systems multiply and interconnect (feeder models, fine-tuned variants, third-party foundation models).

SS1/23 Principle 1SR 11-7 InventoryNIST Map

3. Development, Data & Testing

Standards for how models are built and tested — data suitability, design objectives, and challenger-model comparison. The dimension most disrupted by technology era: deterministic models have fixed logic; ML models are trained/retrained; LLMs are typically fine-tuned or prompted on top of third-party foundation models the bank did not build.

SS1/23 Principle 3SR 11-7 Pillar 1EU AI Act Art.10NIST Measure

4. Independent Validation & Effective Challenge

An organisationally independent function that critically tests suitability, soundness, and robustness before and during use. LLMs stress this dimension hardest — traditional back-testing assumes stable, reproducible outputs; LLM outputs are non-deterministic and validation must adapt (e.g. adversarial/red-team testing, hallucination-rate testing).

SS1/23 Principle 4SR 11-7 "Effective Challenge"EU AI Act Art.15NIST Measure/Manage

5. Monitoring, Mitigants & Human Oversight

Ongoing performance monitoring, post-model adjustments/overrides, restrictions on use, and — increasingly explicit in AI-specific regulation — human oversight of automated decisions. Folds in EU AI Act Article 14 (human oversight), which SS1/23 addresses only implicitly via model owner/user accountability.

SS1/23 Principle 5EU AI Act Art.9 & Art.14NIST ManageNIST GenAI Profile
Note on originality: none of the four grounding sources organise themselves by model-technology era (deterministic/ML/LLM) — each is organised by lifecycle stage or governance function instead. The 5-dimension × 3-era grid above is this project's own analytical overlay, not a restatement of any single regulation. This is the framework's differentiated value for the client.

1. Governance & Accountability No formal MRM policy; model risk handled case-by-case; no board visibility. MRM policy covers traditional models only; SMF assigned but ML/AI use runs as ungoverned "shadow" activity. Firm-wide MRM policy covers all quantitative models incl. ML; board risk appetite set; SMF accountable per SS1/23. Policy explicitly extended to AI/GenAI; board gets aggregate model+AI risk reporting; EU AI Act Art.9(9) integration in place. Unified risk appetite spans deterministic/ML/LLM; policy updates dynamically as GenAI capability shifts; board horizon-scans emerging risk.
2. Inventory & Risk Classification No central inventory; models tracked informally (spreadsheets, tribal knowledge). Inventory covers regulatory-capital models only; ML models largely untracked ("shadow AI"). Firm-wide inventory covers all models incl. ML, with risk-based tiering (materiality × complexity). Inventory extended to GenAI/LLM: third-party foundation models and prompt-configurations tracked; interdependencies mapped. Real-time, largely automated inventory with continuous re-tiering as models/prompts change; full lineage across all three eras.
3. Development, Data & Testing Ad hoc coding; no documented data testing or design objectives. Documented standards for traditional models; ML uses basic train/test splits; no LLM-specific standards exist. SS1/23-aligned development testing (data quality, challenger models) applied consistently to traditional + ML models. Testing extended to GenAI: data provenance/bias checks for fine-tuning, prompt-testing, evaluation harnesses for LLM outputs (EU AI Act Art.10). Continuous automated evaluation pipelines across all model types; LLM-specific suites (hallucination rate, robustness, drift) run pre- and post-deployment.
4. Independent Validation & Effective Challenge No independent validation function; developers self-certify their own models. Independent validation exists for regulatory-capital models only. Validation extended firm-wide incl. ML, per SS1/23 Principle 4 / SR 11-7 "effective challenge". Methods adapted for ML/LLM: statistical back-testing supplemented with red-teaming, adversarial testing, explainability review (EU AI Act Art.15). Continuous validation embedded in CI/CD ("validation-as-code"); independent challenge scales with model velocity without becoming a bottleneck.
5. Monitoring, Mitigants & Human Oversight No structured performance monitoring; issues surface reactively, after incidents. Basic monitoring for traditional models; post-model adjustments applied informally, undocumented. SS1/23-aligned monitoring (benchmarking, override analysis) + formal PMA governance across model types. Human oversight formally designed into ML/LLM workflows (human-in-the-loop for high-risk decisions, per EU AI Act Art.14); drift/hallucination-rate tracked. Adaptive, real-time monitoring with automated circuit-breakers/kill-switches for LLM systems; human oversight calibrated by risk tier, not blanket-applied.
Reading the grid: most banks today sit around L2-L3 for deterministic/traditional models but effectively reset to L1-L2 the moment ML or LLM use cases are introduced, because governance/inventory/validation processes built for deterministic models don't automatically extend to them. The gap between a bank's "headline" maturity and its true AI-inclusive maturity is often the single most useful board-level insight this framework produces.

Loop 3 — Regulatory Mapping

Each dimension mapped against the four grounding sources. PRA SS1/23 and Fed/OCC SR 11-7 are principles-based, technology-neutral, and lifecycle-organised — neither names GenAI/LLMs. EU AI Act is binding law, organised around high-risk AI systems. NIST AI RMF is voluntary, function-organised, and the only source with an LLM-specific extension (GenAI Profile).

Dimension PRA SS1/23 SR 11-7 (Fed/OCC) EU AI Act NIST AI RMF
1. Governance & Accountability Principle 2 — board sets model risk appetite; accountable SMF(s); board-approved policy. Pillar 3 — board/senior management accountability for the MRM framework. Art.9(9) — AI risk management must integrate into, not duplicate, existing (banking) risk management systems. Govern — cross-cutting function; risk-aware culture, policy, roles/accountability.
2. Inventory & Risk Classification Principle 1 — model definition, firm-wide inventory, risk-based tiering (materiality × complexity). Explicit inventory requirement; recommends assessing model risk in aggregate incl. data lineage. Annex III — explicit high-risk classification list (incl. creditworthiness/credit-scoring AI). Map — context, stakeholders, system boundaries, potential impacts.
3. Development, Data & Testing Principle 3 — purpose/design, data suitability, development testing, challenger-model comparison. Pillar 1 — sound development/implementation/use; documentation of data, theory, testing. Art.10 — training data must be representative, bias-assessed, with auditable validation logs. Measure — quantitative/qualitative assessment of trustworthiness (validity, fairness, explainability).
4. Independent Validation & Effective Challenge Principle 4 — independent validation function, organisationally separate from development. Pillar 2 — "effective challenge": critical analysis by objective, informed parties. Art.15 — accuracy, robustness & cybersecurity, incl. resilience to adversarial manipulation. Measure/Manage — testing against trustworthiness characteristics; response to identified gaps.
5. Monitoring, Mitigants & Human Oversight Principle 5 — post-model adjustments, restrictions on use, exceptions & escalation. Ongoing performance monitoring implicit across the full model lifecycle. Art.9 — continuous post-market monitoring; Art.14 — human oversight (most explicit of the four sources). Manage — prioritise/respond to risk; GenAI Profile adds hallucination/drift-specific actions.
Where the four sources converge: all four organise around the same underlying logic — govern → identify → build/test → independently challenge → monitor/mitigate — regardless of whether they call it a "lifecycle" (SS1/23, SR 11-7) or a "function" (NIST). This convergence is what lets a single 5-dimension framework map cleanly to all four.

Where they diverge (the client-relevant gap): SS1/23 and SR 11-7 are technology-neutral by design — neither mentions foundation models, fine-tuning, prompting, or hallucination, and both pre-date mainstream LLM adoption in banking. EU AI Act is the only binding source and the only one to legislate human oversight as its own requirement (Art.14). NIST's GenAI Profile is the only source purpose-built for generative AI risk. Practical implication for the client: a bank that is SS1/23-compliant for its traditional/ML models is not thereby EU AI Act or NIST GenAI-Profile compliant once it adopts LLMs — this is the regulatory gap the maturity framework is designed to surface and close.

Dimension Deterministic — Affordability scorecard ML — Fraud detection model LLM — Complaint-triage assistant
1. Governance & Accountability Owned by Credit Risk; signed off annually by the Retail Credit Committee. Joint ownership, Fraud Ops + Data Science; CRO's model risk appetite statement explicitly extended to cover ML. Cross-functional AI governance forum (Risk, Legal, Compliance, CX) approves the use case; GenAI risk appetite is a standing board agenda item.
2. Inventory & Risk Classification Inventory entry: Tier 2 (moderate materiality, low complexity). Entry adds training-data version, feature-store dependencies, retraining cadence; tiered Tier 1 given fraud-loss materiality. Entry captures the vendor foundation model, prompt-template version, fine-tuning status, RAG data sources; tiered high-risk-adjacent given customer-facing use.
3. Development, Data & Testing Fixed rule weights from policy; one-page methodology note; static back-test vs historical arrears. Trained on 3 years of transaction data; held-out test set; precision/recall tracked; SHAP feature-importance documented. Fine-tuned/prompted atop a vendor foundation model; evaluated on held-out complaint transcripts for accuracy, tone, hallucination rate; red-teamed for jailbreak/prompt-injection pre-go-live.
4. Independent Validation & Effective Challenge Independent Validation reviews rule logic and score cut-offs annually; reports to the Model Risk Committee. Validation independently re-runs the training pipeline, checks for data leakage and bias across protected characteristics, benchmarks vs a simpler challenger model. Adversarial/red-team testing (prompt injection, jailbreaks); sampled live-transcript review for hallucination/inaccuracy; vendor's own model/system card assessed as third-party evidence.
5. Monitoring, Mitigants & Human Oversight Quarterly override analysis; a documented, time-bound PMA for a known seasonal effect. Automated drift monitor alerts on score-distribution shift; monthly dashboard to Fraud Ops; defined recalibration trigger. Real-time guardrail layer blocks disallowed outputs; responses above a risk threshold route to human review before sending; weekly hallucination-rate KPI with an automatic kill-switch on breach.
Pattern across the row: the control activity itself barely changes name (sign-off, inventory entry, testing, validation, monitoring) — what changes is who's involved, how automated the check is, and how quickly it has to run. LLM-era controls in particular shift from periodic/batch (quarterly, monthly) to continuous/real-time, and introduce categories with no deterministic-era equivalent at all (red-teaming, kill-switches, hallucination-rate KPIs).

H-01 — No "how to use this" / target-state guidance

A CRO can score their bank against the grid, but the deliverable doesn't yet say how to turn a score into an action: is L3 an acceptable target for a lower-risk-appetite bank, or should every dimension aim for L4-L5 once ML/LLM is in scope? Missing: guidance on setting a target maturity per dimension and a basic gap-to-target narrative. Recommend addressing before client presentation — either a short "how to use this framework" panel, or explicitly scope it as a Phase 2 follow-on engagement.

M-01 — Vendor/foundation-model concentration risk understated

Dimension 2 and 3 mention tracking third-party foundation models, but the framework doesn't call out concentration risk (a bank's LLM risk profile depends heavily on a small number of foundation-model vendors) as its own board-level concern. Minor — worth a callout, not a blocking issue.

M-02 — Data privacy alongside bias

EU AI Act Art.10 coverage focuses on bias/representativeness; data privacy (GDPR interplay, especially for RAG sources drawing on customer data) isn't explicitly named. Worth a footnote given firm-wide/all-model-types scope.

M-03 — Secondary-source citations for 3 of 4 frameworks

SR 11-7, EU AI Act, and NIST AI RMF content in this deliverable was researched via web search, not read from primary legal/regulatory text (SS1/23 is the exception — read in full from your uploaded PDF). Fine for a first client draft; flag transparently and fetch primary sources before anything goes into a formal, citable client report.
