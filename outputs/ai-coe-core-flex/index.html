Act I

Adoption is easy.
Control is the scarce thing.

Where UK firms are, what the FCA's Mills Review signals, and what has not changed.

86%

of UK financial firms surveyed by the Bank of England and FCA in 2026 already use AI (196 firms).

80% of their agents only support decisions; 2% have broad autonomy. Only 35% of critical, hard-to-substitute cloud relationships have a tested exit plan.

Pilots are cheap. Ownership, redesign and governance are what scale.

5%Value at scale

21%Agent governance

have mature governance for agents (Deloitte, 2026), while about three quarters plan to deploy them.

Governance is not only a brake. Gartner found that organisations running regular AI assessments were about three times as likely to report high GenAI value (Nov 2025). The CoE is where that discipline lives.

The Mills Review, 6 July 2026: the human moves from doing the work to setting the boundaries

L1Operator

Human does the task with AI help.

L2Collaborator

Human and AI work the task together.

L3Consultant

AI does the task; human is consulted.

L4Approver

AI acts; human approves or vetoes.

L5Observer

AI acts; human monitors outcomes.

Role names per the Review's five-role spectrum; one-line glosses are ours. The Review expects not every use case to reach L5. Its seven recommendations are for the FCA Board, so they create no direct firm obligations. The Review judged SM&CR robust at lower autonomy, with pressure at Approver and Observer, and sought no change to Consumer Duty.

You can buy the model.
You cannot buy the accountability.

What has not changed
The FCA plans no AI-specific rules. It relies on existing duties.
  • Consumer Duty and SM&CR apply to AI as to any other tool; SYSC is a benchmark whose scope for this firm type is unconfirmed
  • Firms may outsource AI but remain responsible for outcomes
  • FCA good practice points to mapping AI responsibilities across SMFs, with one senior person accountable for overall oversight
What the FCA expects to see
Governance at design stage, not after deployment.
  • Real human oversight, not a tick box
  • Records of prompts, outputs, model versions and overrides
  • Visibility of supplier model changes, plus contingency plans

Act II

Client, Core and Flex.
The client keeps the keys.

The client is accountable. Our Core is committed. Our Flex is elastic. Control is built in, not only written into the contract.

Core: ours, committed

Leads.
Runs.
Evidences.

A fixed team of key delivery roles: delivery lead, architect, platform engineer, the embedded FDE team and a separate evaluation pod. It runs the platform and keeps the evidence for the client.

Flex: ours, elastic

Builds.
Flexes.
Dissolves.

Pods we scale up and down against the client's backlog: build, evaluation and Academy delivery. Capped call-offs with a mission and an expiry. Runbooks and tests stay behind.

What the client keeps: every accountable role

  1. Executive sponsor

    Gives the mandate and budget and takes AI to the board.

    Sponsors
  2. Accountable executive

    A named senior manager, recorded in their Statement of Responsibilities, owns AI oversight and signs higher-tier releases. Which SMF is the firm's choice: no FCA statement names one.

    Decides
  3. Compliance and governance

    Tiers every use case, approves acceptance criteria, reads the evaluation pod's findings directly and attests before release. Compliance, Legal and the DPO review at set gates.

    Assures
  4. Product owner

    Holds requirements and priorities, approves each Flex call-off and its spend, and signs off outcomes in the ledger.

    Directs
  5. Business users

    Use AI at the level they are licensed for, review outputs and sign them off where the licence says so.

    Use and sign off

Control is structural: the client owns the tenancy Core and Flex work in

Design choice, our judgement. Fits the FCA's outsourcing expectations to supervise, terminate and retain access, though SYSC 8's applicability to this firm type is unconfirmed. Exit becomes a credential revocation, not a negotiation.

How it is bought: fixed Core, capped Flex, a small slice for outcomes

  1. Core retainer

    Our committed key roles: delivery lead, architect, platform engineer, evaluation pod and embedded FDE team. Fixed, because continuity and evidence cannot be a variable cost. The evaluation pod is kept separate from the build pods.

    Fixed
  2. Flex call-off

    Pods scale up when the ledger shows evidenced backlog and the client's product owner approves, within a pod limit and the monthly cap. Scale down on 30 days notice (assumption) or at the stage gate.

    Capped
  3. At-risk slice, 10-15%

    Released against outcomes verified in the client's own ledger. Our judgement on the share.

    Incentive
  4. Consumption at cost

    Model and token spend passed through with a cap, so agent costs cannot drift unseen.

    Pass-through
Why the incentive stays small

Bain finds only about one in ten hybrid AI meters is outcome-based. Finance and HR productivity is hard to attribute, so most of the fee should not hang on it.

          Act IV

          The governance layer.
          Built once, reused for every use case.

          A tiering gate and evidence by tier. UK duties bind today; the EU AI Act is the design benchmark.

          One gate sorts every use case into a tier

          T0Internal productivity

          No personal data on customers or staff, and no decisions about people.

          Inventory entry, owner, acceptable-use acknowledgement, vendor terms check.

          T1Transparency

          Customer-facing or generated content. Personal data, but no decision on a person.

          Adds Art 50 disclosure design, DPIA screen, Consumer Duty check, test log.

          T2Assistive on people

          Supports a human decision about customers or staff. Run as if high-risk-lite.

          Adds risk assessment, bias and accuracy test report, oversight procedure with override and sampling, and SMF-owner sign-off.

          T3High-risk equivalent

          Hiring, performance, or affordability scoring of individuals. Stop-and-review.

          Adds a DPIA, plus a FRIA where Art 27 applies (such as credit scoring), oversight authorisation, worker and affected-person notices, and board visibility (our addition).

          Tiers and artefacts are our design, judgement-based. Any prohibited-practice hit stops the use case. Hits include emotion recognition on staff.

          When the EU AI Act bites on a UK mortgage firm

          2 Feb 2025In force

          Prohibited practices and the AI literacy duty. The Omnibus eased the literacy duty: no per-person sufficiency test. A documented programme is our proposed evidence.

          27 Jul 2026In force

          Digital Omnibus, Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, delays the high-risk dates. We did not read the Official Journal text: verify before client use.

          2 Aug 2026In force

          Transparency duties for chatbots and generated content (Art 50). A grace period for marking generative output on the market before this date runs to 2 Dec 2026.

          2 Dec 2027Upcoming

          High-risk duties for Annex III, including employment and credit scoring.

          2 Aug 2028Upcoming

          High-risk duties for AI embedded in regulated products.

          Reach

          A UK-only firm with no EU customers, staff or entities is largely outside the Act. It bites where AI output is used in the EU (Art 2(1)(c)). Consumer Duty, SM&CR and UK GDPR are the binding drivers; the AI Act is the benchmark and the contract flow-down.

          What the time-saved case is worth only if cash levers are named

          Levers that turn time into cash
          Each needs an owner and a target in the ledger.
          • Invoice and reconciliation automation (F1, F2)
          • Procuration-fee leakage recovery (F5): potentially direct cash, size unknown
          • Vacancy avoidance, and contractor or overtime reduction
          • Redeployed capacity with a named destination
          Why not assume it
          Independent evidence shows time saved is not cash saved.
          • UK government Copilot trial: 26 minutes a day, self-reported; use of saved time unknown
          • DBT evaluation: no robust evidence that time savings improved productivity
          • Gartner HR survey: 88% of HR leaders have not realised significant value
          • McKinsey: only 39% report any EBIT impact, mostly under 5%
